# L2.2 — The Decision Memo

**Lesson 2, second block.** About 30 minutes. Turn the analysis into something an executive will act
on.

## The idea

Your reader will spend ninety seconds on this. They'll read the first paragraph carefully and the
rest selectively.

Everything follows from that. **The recommendation goes first** — not the background, not the
method, not a narrative of how the analysis unfolded. If they read only the opening paragraph, they
should be able to act correctly.

Most people write these backwards, in the order the work happened. It feels natural and it wastes
the only paragraph you're guaranteed to have read.

## What you do

Open `L2.2-Decision-Memo-Template.md` and start the `decision-memo` skill: *"write this up for my
VP."*

Fill the reader block at the top of the template first. Who reads this, what they already believe,
what they optimize for, and whether they can decide alone. The same analysis produces a genuinely
different memo for a CFO than for an operations director, and it's not a matter of tone — it's what
goes in the first paragraph.

## The two things most memos leave out

**The strongest counterargument.** Include it, in the evidence section, on its own. This feels like
weakening your case and does the opposite. A reader who finds the counterargument themselves,
afterward, discounts everything else you wrote.

**What the analysis couldn't determine.** Data that didn't exist, a window too short, a comparison
that was confounded. Stating limitations plainly signals that you know where the edges are — and it
prevents the far worse outcome of someone else finding a limitation you didn't disclose.

## The four tests

Run all four before you're done. The template has them as a checklist.

1. **Ninety-second test** — read only the recommendation paragraph. Could they act correctly on it?
2. **Skeptic test** — the reader's sharpest colleague reads it. What's their first question? Is it
   answered?
3. **Forward test** — six months from now the recommendation was followed and it went badly.
   Someone pulls up this memo. Does it hold? Did it name the risk that materialized?
4. **Hostile-quote test** — which single sentence would someone pull out of context to misrepresent
   the analysis? Rewrite it so they can't.

## One page

400–500 words for the memo proper. Detail goes in an appendix or stays in the analysis document and
gets referenced. If you're over 600, it isn't finished yet.

## If the answer is "we don't know"

Write that memo. "The analysis was inconclusive, here's what would settle it, here's what I
recommend doing in the meantime" is a real recommendation and a considerably better one than a
confident answer the evidence doesn't support.
